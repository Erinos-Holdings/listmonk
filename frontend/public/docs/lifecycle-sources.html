One Journey, Every Brand

Proposal: one engagement journey standard on every brand (11 steps since 2026-11-06, when Marketing dropped the welcome Day 10).

The ask

Adopt one journey skeleton for every brand, vary only the copy and the offers, and start with data-proven, industry-backed intervals. Change an interval only when a brand's own numbers prove it should change. The rest of this page is the case for that, including defensible alternatives.

9 of 12intervals sit on a Klaviyo default or published recommendation; the other 3 are deliberate, stated extensions

Why one standard rather than a path per brand

No single brand can test its own intervals

To tell a 2.0% conversion step from a 2.5% one (a 25% lift) at normal confidence takes about 14,000 people per arm. Our brands sign up far fewer than that in a month, so a per-brand interval test would run for years before it said anything. Pooled across brands, the same test finishes in months.

The industry already standardizes

Klaviyo ships one pre-built Welcome, Post-purchase and Sunset flow to every account, and its "three emails over a week" welcome guidance comes from analysis across thousands of its ecommerce customers, not per-store tuning. Brands differentiate on copy, product and offer. The skeleton is shared because the behaviour it responds to (a signup cooling off, a parcel arriving, a reader going quiet) is the same for a pouch brand and a skincare brand.

Comparability is the whole point of Brand Usage

The Usage pane scores every brand on the same leaves and the Matrix compares them side by side. That only means something if Day 5 is Day 5 everywhere. With a standard, a result on one brand is a lesson for all of them; with per-brand paths, every brand is a sample of one and nothing transfers.

It is also the only way the copy stays maintainable: today's standard is already 12 steps × up to 5 languages per brand.

The six recommended modifications

Each one is a frequency or timing fix, and each has a number behind it.

  1. Skip the Welcome for someone whose signup is their first order; send only the Thank-you.Item 3.Last 28 days: about 20% of Thirsty Girl and 10% of Ruze signups got both emails back to back. Frequency is the top or second unsubscribe reason in every consumer survey (46% GetApp, 35% BlueHornet, 20% Mailgun).
  2. Keep step emails evergreen and distinct from the month's launches.Item 4.Nothing suppresses a broadcast mid-series. Distinct copy means an overlap is two different emails, not a duplicate, and it is the only way the step copy stays maintainable across brands and languages.
  3. Retire Ruze's 15-day step and re-schedule its campaign as Day 2.Item 7.Every Klaviyo recommendation ends the welcome inside a week; the copy already reads as the gentle reminder.
  4. Start the post-purchase clock at delivery, order date + 5 as the fallback.Item 9.Klaviyo's review flow triggers on Fulfilled Order for the same reason. Never ask about a product still in transit.
  5. No cross-brand cap yet; skip, never defer, if it is ever needed.Item 12.4.7% of active subscribers (1,185 of 25,045) are on two or more brands; 0.8% on three. Revisit past ~15%. Klaviyo Smart Sending has no cross-brand awareness at all.
  6. Per-brand Smart Sending: skip a broadcast to anyone who got that brand's journey email in the last 24 h.Item 13.Without it a new non-buyer can get seven emails in ten days. Klaviyo's default is 16 hours and a skip. GetApp: 56% of US consumers unsubscribe at four or more messages from one company in 30 days.

Re-engagement: why 180 days, not 90

Ninety days is the common starting point and we are not disputing it. Klaviyo's default engaged segments are 30, 60 and 90 days, and its staff advise 90 for brands that send weekly or monthly. We hold at 180 for four reasons, and we keep the ability to move back to 90 per brand without a deploy.

1 · Our engagement test is stricter than the one 90 days assumes

Most 90-day segments count any open. Apple Mail Privacy Protection fires opens automatically, inflating open rates by a reported 20–40%; Klaviyo's own advice is to exclude those opens. We go further: only a click, a paid order or a Gmail-confirmed open counts. A subscriber judged on that bar deserves more calendar time, and Klaviyo's never-engaged sunset segment itself uses 180 days.

2 · Purging fast loses real readers

Return Path's study of 33 retailer win-back programmes (300M emails) found 45% of win-back recipients went on to read later messages, on average 57 days after the win-back, some up to 300 days later. Only 12% read the win-back itself. A 90-day cut plus a short sunset would have removed many of them.

3 · The Younique win-back is our own data point

A lapsed audience still contained a reading third. It also showed the limit: once the day-4 FBL complaint rate on a tranche passed 0.1%, sending was paused the same day and the campaign cancelled, with 26,759 unreached people archived rather than mailed. Both halves of that argue for 180 with a hair trigger rather than 90 without one.

4 · We see trouble days before Google reports it

Postmaster Tools lags two to three days. We do not wait for it: FBL complaints and hard, soft and block-class bounces per campaign the same day; per-client view rates on every send, so a Gmail drop against a steady Yahoo is visible before Postmaster moves; daily Brand Health per brand (Postmaster compliance and spam rate, SES account reputation, blocklists), trust-tiered open counts, and a sunset run that skips a brand whose tracking looks broken. The 180-day window is a per-brand registry value: shortening one brand to 90 is a one-line change, not a release.

Our commitment: if a brand's Gmail spam rate sits above 0.10% across two consecutive Brand Health reads, or an FBL complaint rate passes 0.1% on two broadcasts in a month, that brand's hold window drops to 90 days and we report it here. The window is liberal because the exits are fast.

Defensible alternatives

These are the positions a well-read challenger would take. Each is reasonable; here is where we stand on each.

Three welcome emails, not four (Day 0 · 3 · 7). Adopted 2026-11-06.

This is Klaviyo's default and its best-supported recommendation. Our Day 10 nudge added a conversion attempt the evidence did not demand; this page called it the interval we would concede first, and Marketing conceded it: the No-purchase series is Day 0 · 2 · 5 on every brand.

Review ask timed by product category.

Several practitioners argue consumables want a 7-day ask and durable goods 14–21. We agree in principle, but it is a copy decision inside a standard step rather than a reason for per-brand intervals; a delivery-based clock (modification 4) removes most of the error.

A 90-day hold for high-frequency brands.

Klaviyo advises 30 days for daily senders and 90 for weekly. If a brand moves to several broadcasts a week, 180 is too generous for it and the registry window should come down. Nothing in the plan prevents that.

Smart Sending that also suppresses journey steps.

Klaviyo's pre-built flows have Smart Sending on by default, so a flow email can be skipped after a campaign. We exempt journeys because each step sends once, ever, and a skipped step is a lost step. If Marketing prefers Klaviyo's behaviour, it is the same rule with journeys included.

Fewer, earlier re-engagement asks.

Some teams compress the sunset to three asks within 30 days and suppress. That is cheaper to run. We prefer spreading the asks across the back half of the year because of the Return Path finding that re-reads arrive months later, and because the 360/365 lists are locked to automations so nobody past day 360 can be promoted to by mistake.

Revising the standard

We would revise an interval, not just a copy line, on any of these: a per-brand A/B with at least 14,000 subscribers per arm showing a different step wins; a Day 10 unsubscribe share consistently above the Day 5 share; the welcome series' Day 2 or Day 5 clicks falling below the broadcast average for a quarter; or a held-at-180 cohort whose re-engagement asks draw complaints above 0.1%. Bring any of those and the standard moves for every brand at once, which is the point of having one.
